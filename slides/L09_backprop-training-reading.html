Slide 3

Past the XOR wall: stack neurons into layers

  • One neuron draws one line: at best 3 of the 4 XOR points (left). The perceptron never reaches zero mistakes on XOR
  • With two hidden units (neurons between the input and the output), the output can fire in the band between two lines and get all 4 (right). Stacked neurons draw boundaries that one line cannot

The network that solves XOR

  • aj[l]a^{[l]}_j is the output of unit jj in layer ll. Hidden unit 1 acts like OR (on when any input is on): a1[1]=ReLU(x1+x2)a^{[1]}_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(x_1+x_2). Unit 2: a2[1]=ReLU(x1+x2−1)a^{[1]}_2 = \mathrm{ReLU}(x_1+x_2-1). Which function? (minute paper)
  • The output fires if a1[1]−2a2[1]>0.5a^{[1]}_1 - 2a^{[1]}_2 > 0.5: OR but not unit 2, so XOR. Can a network learn these weights?

Slide 7

Backpropagation: a long and disputed history

  • 1969: Minsky & Papert (1969) show that a perceptron cannot compute XOR. No widely known method trains hidden layers, and interest in neural networks fades
  • 1970–1974: Linnainmaa, studying rounding errors in computer programs, computes all the derivatives of a long calculation in one backward sweep. Werbos derives it for general models in 1974 and applies it to neural networks in 1982
  • 1986: Rumelhart et al. (1986) show that hidden units trained this way learn useful internal representations. Their Nature paper made backprop the standard method
  • Who deserves the credit is still argued. Hinton's 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Hopfield, cited a different kind of network, the Boltzmann machine, not backprop. His 2018 Turing Award, shared with Bengio and LeCun, was for deep learning as a whole
Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams Nature 1986 · NobelPrize.org Physics 2024 · ACM Turing Award 2018

  • A hidden unit has no target: what should a1[1]a^{[1]}_1 have been? This is credit assignment

Slide 9

How should each weight change?

  • Compare the output with its target. With the cross-entropy loss, the error at the output is y^−y=0.54−1=−0.46\hat y - y = 0.54 - 1 = -0.46. Negative: the output should go up
  • The goal: change the weights a little, so that next time the output lands closer to its target
  • For each weight, ask: if it grew slightly, would the loss ℓ\ell of this example go up or down, and how fast? That slope is the gradient. Step against it: w←w−η ∂ℓ/∂ww \leftarrow w - \eta\,\partial\ell/\partial w
  • A weight into the output gets the output error directly. A hidden unit acts only through the units it feeds; its error is the output error sent back along that connection, times the connection's weight (and 0 if the unit was off)
  • The math behind it is the chain rule of calculus (the slope of a function of a function is the product of the slopes). Not derived in class (see the backpropagation notes). The next slides run it with numbers
  • Activity flows forward to the output. Error flows backward to every weight

Slide 11

Step 1: the forward pass

  • Run the input through and keep every weighted sum zz and output aa for the backward pass
  • Hidden: z1[1]=0.50⋅1−0.40⋅0=0.50z^{[1]}_1 = 0.50\cdot1 - 0.40\cdot0 = 0.50, a1[1]=ReLU(0.50)=0.50a^{[1]}_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(0.50) = 0.50; z2[1]=0.30⋅1+0.80⋅0=0.30=a2[1]z^{[1]}_2 = 0.30\cdot1 + 0.80\cdot0 = 0.30 = a^{[1]}_2. Output: z[2]=0.70⋅0.50−0.60⋅0.30=0.17z^{[2]} = 0.70\cdot0.50 - 0.60\cdot0.30 = 0.17, y^=σ(0.17)=0.54\hat y = \sigma(0.17) = 0.54 (σ\sigma: sigmoid)
  • Its loss, the cross-entropy: ℓ=−log⁡y^=−log⁡0.542≈0.61\ell = -\log \hat y = -\log 0.542 \approx 0.61

Slide 12

Step 2: the error at the output

  • Sigmoid + cross-entropy: error = prediction − target: δ[2]=∂ℓ/∂z[2]=y^−y=−0.46\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\delta^{[2]}} = \partial\ell/\partial z^{[2]} = \hat y - y = -0.46
  • Negative: the output was too low and should rise. Every later step builds on this output error

Slide 13

Step 3: send the error backward

  • A hidden unit's error = output error × the weight linking them × its ReLU slope g′g' (1 if its weighted sum zz was positive, else 0)
  • δ1[1]=δ[2] w1[2] g′(z1[1])=−0.46×0.70×1=−0.32\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\delta^{[1]}_1} = \textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\delta^{[2]}}\,\textcolor{#B5396B}{w^{[2]}_1}\,g'(z^{[1]}_1) = -0.46\times0.70\times1 = -0.32; δ2[1]=−0.46×(−0.60)×1≈0.27\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\delta^{[1]}_2} = -0.46\times(-0.60)\times1 \approx 0.27
  • Each hidden unit gets its own error, even its own sign: a1[1]a^{[1]}_1 should have been higher, a2[1]a^{[1]}_2 lower

Slide 14

Step 4: every gradient is error × activity

  • gradient = error × activity: ∂ℓ/∂w1[2]=−0.46×0.50=−0.23\partial\ell/\partial \textcolor{#B5396B}{w^{[2]}_1} = \textcolor{#3F7A6B}{-0.46}\times\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{0.50} = -0.23
  • Green numbers: the six gradients. Weights from x2x_2 get 0 (x2=0x_2 = 0). Each bias (input always 1) gets its unit's error: −0.32,0.27,−0.46-0.32, 0.27, -0.46
  • One step w←w−η ∂ℓ/∂ww \leftarrow w - \eta\,\partial\ell/\partial w with η=1\eta = 1 on all six weights and three biases: y^\hat y rises from 0.54 to 0.82

Slide 15

Why it works: the chain rule, run backward

  • The loss depends on the weight w11[1]w^{[1]}_{11} (from x1x_1 to a1[1]a^{[1]}_1) through a chain: w11[1]→z1[1]→a1[1]→z[2]→ℓw^{[1]}_{11}\to z^{[1]}_1\to a^{[1]}_1\to z^{[2]}\to\ell. The chain rule multiplies the local slopes along it, as steps 2 to 4 did
  • Backprop is the chain rule of multivariable calculus, applied layer by layer. You will not be asked to derive it. If you have taken multivariable calculus, the derivation is in the backpropagation notes

Slide 18

Many classes: softmax and cross-entropy

p^c=ezc∑jezj,ℓ=−log⁡p^true class\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\hat p}_c = \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}, \qquad \ell = -\log \textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\hat p}_{\text{true class}}

  • One weighted sum per class cc, the logits zcz_c. The softmax turns them into probabilities; the loss: −log of the true class's probability
  • Example: logits (2,1,0)(2, 1, 0) give 𝐩^=(0.67,0.24,0.09)\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\hat{\mathbf p}} = (0.67, 0.24, 0.09). True class 1: ℓ=0.41\ell = 0.41. True class 3: ℓ=2.41\ell = 2.41
  • Right and confident, ℓ→0\ell \to 0; wrong and confident, ℓ\ell grows without bound

Slide 20

One training step, and one epoch

  • One step: a batch (a set of examples processed together) forward, one number ℒ (their average loss), its gradient for every weight, then every weight moves a little
  • One epoch: every example in the training set once. Training repeats epochs while ℒ falls

Slide 21

The training loop

model = nn.Linear(D, K) # D inputs, one logit for each of K classes loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss() # softmax + cross-entropy, averaged opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1) # SGD: a plain gradient step for epoch in range(num_epochs): # one epoch = one pass over the training set opt.zero_grad() # 1. clear the old gradients logits = model(X_train) # 2. forward: logits for every image loss = loss_fn(logits, y_train) # 3. the average cross-entropy loss.backward() # 4. gradient of the loss for every weight opt.step() # 5. update: w <- w - lr * gradient with torch.no_grad(): # evaluate on held-out images acc = (model(X_test).argmax(1) == y_test).float().mean()
  • This model is a linear readout. To train a hidden layer, change only the model line
  • backward() computes the gradients. opt, the optimizer, turns them into weight changes in step()
  • This is the delta rule's loop, written for a whole layer: forward, loss, gradient, step, repeat

Slide 22

Autograd: you never write the backward pass

  • PyTorch's automatic differentiation (autograd) records the forward pass as a computational graph: the operations that ran, and the values each one used
  • loss.backward() sweeps the graph in reverse: output error, hidden errors, then each parameter's gradient, stored in its .grad. opt.step() applies the update
Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul & Siskind JMLR 2018

Slide 23

Train only part of a network

model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(D, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, K)) model.load_state_dict(torch.load("trained.pt")) # weights trained earlier for p in model[0].parameters(): # the hidden layer p.requires_grad = False # no gradients: these weights stay fixed opt = torch.optim.Adam(model[2].parameters(), lr=1e-3) # Adam: an optimizer, defined later
  • To freeze a trained layer, turn off its gradients and give the optimizer only the weights that should learn
  • Readout only: cheap. It tells you what a trained readout can find in the hidden layer's responses, which stay fixed
  • Every weight: backpropagation also changes the hidden layer, so its responses to the images change too
  • Same data, same loss: only the set of weights allowed to move differs

Slide 25

Minibatches: a gradient from a few examples at a time

  • Full batch: the gradient over all NN training examples at every step. Exact, but slow for large NN
  • Minibatch: shuffle the data, then take one step per batch of BB examples (often 32 to 256). One step is also called an iteration; an epoch now holds many steps
  • Each minibatch gradient is a noisy estimate of the full one. It is much cheaper, and the noise can help the weights escape shallow dips in the loss
  • With B=1B = 1 this is the original stochastic gradient descent (the SGD of the training-loop code)
Robbins & Monro Ann Math Stat 1951

Slide 26

Shrink the step: learning-rate schedules

  • With noisy minibatch gradients, a constant step keeps bouncing. Too large overshoots, too small crawls
  • A schedule lowers η\eta during training: large steps early, small steps late. Top: the step sizes. Bottom: the losses; dashed, the lowest loss these data allow
  • Step decay halves η\eta at fixed intervals (here every 200 steps). Cosine lowers it smoothly to near 0. 1/(1+t/τ)1/(1+t/\tau) shrinks it a little at every step tt; τ\tau sets how soon the shrinking starts
  • In the last-200-steps inset, the shrinking schedules fluctuate less

Slide 27

Adam: the most popular optimizer

𝐦←β1𝐦+(1−β1) ∇L,𝐯←β2𝐯+(1−β2) (∇L)2,θ←θ−η 𝐦𝐯+ϵ\mathbf{m}\leftarrow\beta_1\mathbf{m}+(1-\beta_1)\,\nabla\mathcal{L},\qquad \mathbf{v}\leftarrow\beta_2\mathbf{v}+(1-\beta_2)\,(\nabla\mathcal{L})^2,\qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta\,\frac{\mathbf{m}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{v}}+\epsilon}

θ: all weights and biases · every operation is done one weight at a time · the small correction Adam applies in its first steps is omitted

  • Momentum (𝐦\mathbf{m}): a running average of recent gradients. The noisy parts of successive minibatch gradients cancel; a consistent direction adds up
  • A step size for each weight (𝐯\mathbf{v}): each weight's step is divided by the typical size of its own gradient. Weights with small gradients still move, and large gradients are scaled down
  • β1\beta_1 and β2\beta_2 set how much of each running average is kept at every step. The defaults, β1=0.9\beta_1=0.9 and β2=0.999\beta_2=0.999, work on most problems. ϵ\epsilon, a tiny number, prevents division by zero
  • Usually fewer steps to a low loss than plain gradient descent, but it still needs a learning rate
Kingma & Ba ICLR 2015 · Loshchilov & Hutter ICLR 2019 · Wilson, Roelofs, Stern, Srebro & Recht NeurIPS 2017

Slide 28

The learning rate, measured

  • Four runs of one network (64 ReLU hidden units) on handwritten digits, differing only in η\eta; "test" = accuracy on held-out images
  • Too large a step can switch a ReLU unit off on every input: a dead unit
  • Too small: the loss barely moves. Too large: the loss sticks at chance (−log 1/10 ≈ 2.3 for ten digits), the units have died
  • The range that works spans orders of magnitude, so sweep η\eta on a log scale, and sweep it first

Slide 29

Recap

  • Hidden layers: one neuron draws one line. Two ReLU hidden units re-represent the XOR inputs so that one line separates the classes
  • Backpropagation: forward pass, error at the output (y^−y\hat y - y), error sent backward through the weights, and every gradient is error × activity. Autograd runs these steps for you
  • The training loop: a softmax readout for many classes, then forward, loss, backward(), step(), repeated over epochs. Freezing a trained layer trains only the readout
  • Step sizes: minibatches, a learning rate that shrinks, and Adam. Sweep η\eta on a log scale first
