Slide 2

Where we left off: one filter, slid over the image

  • A filter, a small grid of weights, a template like a simple cell's receptive field, is slid over the image; at each position the dot product, then ReLU: a feature map
  • One set of weights, reused at every position (weight sharing), so the layer has few weights. Shift the image and the map shifts with it

Slide 3

Where we left off: simple and complex cells

  • Hubel and Wiesel's two cell types in V1: the simple cell, a weighted sum, selective for an oriented bar at one position; the complex cell, which pools simple cells and responds to the same bar anywhere nearby
  • Convolution plays the simple cell (selectivity); max-pooling plays the complex cell (tolerance to position)

Slide 6

Complex cells, again: one orientation, anywhere nearby

  • A complex cell sums five simple cells of one orientation, their fields side by side
  • Whichever simple cell the bar falls on, the complex cell fires
  • Tolerant to position, selective for orientation: this is pooling
Hubel & Wiesel J Physiol 1962

Return to contents

Slide 7

Cortical columns: every orientation, at every position

  • A column: a stack of cells through the six layers of cortex that prefer one orientation. Hubel and Wiesel's ice-cube model: a 1 mm block of V1 holds a column for every orientation, for each eye; the block repeats across the visual field
  • Nothing slides in cortex. Each position has its own cells, with their own synapses (connections, each with a weight). We call this replication; the network uses sharing
  • Both end up with the same template at every position. The network stores one filter; cortex has a separate copy at each position. How those copies come to match is not known
Hubel & Wiesel Proc R Soc B 1977 · Kandel, Schwartz & Jessell Principles of Neural Science 2000

Return to contents

Slide 8

The hierarchy in V1 continues through the ventral stream

  • Within V1, Hubel and Wiesel found a hierarchy: centre–surround → simple → complex, selectivity, then tolerance
  • The ventral stream: V1 → V2 → V4 → IT (inferotemporal cortex), the chain that ends in object recognition. The working assumption: the same two steps repeat in each area
  • Three kinds of evidence: cells prefer more complex things at each stage (next slides); anatomy orders the areas (next slide); responses start later at each stage, about 40 ms at the LGN (the relay between eye and V1) to 100 ms in IT
DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust Neuron 2012 · Schmolesky et al. J Neurophysiol 1998

Return to contents

Slide 9

Anatomy orders the areas: Felleman and Van Essen

  • Felleman & Van Essen (1991) collected the known connections between 32 visual areas, about 300 of them
  • Cortex has six layers, and a connection's direction is written in them: feedforward axons (away from the eye) end in layer 4 of the next area; feedback axons (back toward V1) avoid layer 4
  • Ordering the areas so that every feedforward connection points up gives about ten levels, V1 at the bottom, IT, area 46 and the hippocampal areas at the top: a hierarchy with many paths through it
Felleman & Van Essen Cereb Cortex 1991

Slide 10

V4: beyond bars, non-Cartesian gratings

Drawn from the stimulus formulas; schematic

  • Gallant, Braun & Van Essen (1993) showed V4 cells three families of gratings: the usual Cartesian stripes, polar ones (rings, spokes, spirals) and hyperbolic ones (saddles)
  • Many V4 cells responded best to a polar or hyperbolic grating: no single oriented bar detector prefers these patterns
  • Later studies of V4 found tuning for curvature, colour and texture (next slides)
Gallant, Braun & Van Essen Science 1993 · Gallant et al. J Neurophysiol 1996

Slide 11

What the cells prefer, area by area

  • Kobatake & Tanaka (1994) reduced each cell's best object to the simplest picture that still drove it. Most V2 cells settle for a bar or a spot; the share of cells that need more grows up the stream: curves and textured shapes in V4, parts in posterior IT, faces and hands in anterior IT
Kobatake & Tanaka J Neurophysiol 1994 · Desimone, Albright, Gross & Bruce J Neurosci 1984

Slide 12

V4: curvature at an angular position

  • Pasupathy & Connor (2001) showed V4 cells 51 shapes at 8 rotations and found that a cell's response is set by the curvature of the boundary at one angular position around the shape's centre, a sharp convexity at 45°, say: a part of the outline, not the whole shape
Pasupathy & Connor J Neurophysiol 2001 · Pospisil, Pasupathy & Bair eLife 2018

Slide 13

V2: naturalistic textures

  • Freeman et al. (2013): two synthetic versions of each photograph. Spectrally matched noise keeps the statistics of V1-like filters; naturalistic texture also keeps their correlations
  • V1 responds alike to both; V2 responds more to naturalistic texture, and so do people
Freeman, Ziemba, Heeger, Simoncelli & Movshon Nat Neurosci 2013

Photographs and whole-stream recordings

  • THINGS (Hebart et al. 2019): 1,854 object concepts, 26,107 photographs
  • TVSD (Papale et al. 2025): 22,248 THINGS photographs shown to two monkeys; Utah arrays (10 × 10 grids of electrodes) recorded V1, V4 and IT at the same time
  • Triple-N (Li et al. 2026): Neuropixels probes, hundreds of recording sites on one shank, across IT
  • NSD (Allen et al. 2022): 7 T fMRI in eight people, 73,000 photographs

Slide 15

Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980)

  • S-cells: simple cells, one template per plane (a plane is one feature map), at every position. C-cells: pool nearby S-cells of one plane
  • Repeat S then C; the top responds to a whole digit wherever it sits. Trained without labels, layer by layer
Fukushima Biol Cybern 1980 · Hubel & Wiesel J Physiol 1962

Return to contents

Slide 16

HMAX: a hierarchy constrained by physiology

  • S and C alternate, V1 to IT; pooling by the max, over position and over scale (next slide)
  • Hyperparameters (receptive-field sizes, tuning widths, pooling ranges) set by hand to match physiology, not fit to the task: "graduate student descent"
  • No backprop: S2 units learn by simple unsupervised, Hebbian-like rules, picking up frequent combinations of C1 features (from orientations to corners and curves); only the readout sees labels
Riesenhuber & Poggio Nat Neurosci 1999 · Serre, Oliva & Poggio PNAS 2007 · Serre & Poggio Commun ACM 2010 · Crick Nature 1989

Return to contents

Slide 17

Pooling over position, and over scale

  • HMAX's S1 bank: Gabor filters at 4 orientations and 16 sizes. A C1 unit takes the max over nearby positions and over two adjacent sizes: 8 scale bands
  • So an object seen once at one size drives nearly the same C1 units at another size: HMAX has tolerance to scale built in
  • A network has no built-in scale pooling: its size tolerance comes from the sizes in its training photographs and from data augmentation, the rescaled copies of last lecture, and it fades outside the range it saw
Serre, Wolf, Bileschi, Riesenhuber & Poggio IEEE PAMI 2007

Return to contents

Slide 18

The same two steps, higher up

  • A simple unit above V1 is again a weighted sum, now over complex cells: it is selective for a conjunction of oriented edges at set positions, a corner, a curve, a textured patch
  • A complex unit above it pools those simple units over positions and sizes, so it responds to that conjunction anywhere in its field
  • Repeat a weighted sum, then pooling, and both selectivity and tolerance grow from V2 to V4 to IT
Riesenhuber & Poggio Nat Neurosci 1999 · Serre, Oliva & Poggio PNAS 2007

Return to contents

Slide 19

Pooling: from a map to fewer numbers

  • A map says where a feature is, to the pixel; recognition needs whether, roughly where. Max-pooling keeps the largest value in each window, so a small shift of the feature gives the same answer. This is tolerance to position, as in a complex cell; HMAX's C stages do the same
  • Global average pooling averages each map over all positions: one number per channel (one channel = one filter's feature map); together they form the image's feature vector, the readout's input
Riesenhuber & Poggio Nat Neurosci 1999 · Lin, Chen & Yan ICLR 2014

Return to contents

Slide 20

HMAX worked: predictions from V1 to behaviour

  • One model, one set of parameters, tested from V1 to behaviour
  • Some results were predicted before the data: max-like pooling in V4 and in some V1 complex cells
  • V4 tuning for curved boundaries (Pasupathy & Connor) was matched by fitting model units to each cell; a linear readout of the same model units named the category as Hung et al.'s IT readout did
Serre & Poggio Commun ACM 2010 · Pasupathy & Connor J Neurophysiol 2001 · Hung, Kreiman, Poggio & DiCarlo Science 2005

Slide 21

The test on behaviour: rapid animal categorization

  • Serre, Oliva & Poggio (2007): the animal / non-animal task of the vision lecture's opening movie. Photographs flashed for 20 ms, then a noise mask (a second image that overwrites the first) 50 ms after onset: too soon for feedback from higher areas to return
  • d′d': sensitivity, hits against false alarms, 0 = guessing. The model matched people at every viewing distance and was wrong on the same images more often than two equally good observers would be
  • Conclusion: with an early mask, one feedforward pass is enough
Serre, Oliva & Poggio PNAS 2007 · Thorpe, Fize & Marlot Nature 1996

Slide 22

Then scale took over

  • CNNs existed, LeCun's LeNet (1998), with every filter learned end to end by backprop; through the 2000s they and HMAX were about equally good, and neither got much better with more data
  • What changed in 2012: GPUs, and 1.2 million labelled photographs (ImageNet). AlexNet, then 8 → 152 layers in three years, better every year (top-5: the right label among the five best guesses)
  • AlexNet kept the same stack (a weighted sum, then a max) but set no numbers by hand: all were learned from the labels
LeCun, Bottou, Bengio & Haffner Proc IEEE 1998 · Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton NeurIPS 2012 · He, Zhang, Ren & Sun CVPR 2016

Return to contents

Slide 23

Same operations, different settings and learning

  • Operations: the same. A weighted sum, then a pooling, stacked. HMAX's S and C; a network's convolution and max-pooling
  • Settings: HMAX took every filter size, pooling range and tuning width from physiology. A deep network sets them by an engineering criterion, whatever maximizes accuracy on held-out images, with filter sizes, strides and depth tried by hand
  • Learning: HMAX learned its S2 templates without labels and trained only a readout on top, animal or not. A network learns every weight by backpropagation from 1.2 million labels, 1,000 classes, supervised end to end
  • Scale tolerance: built into HMAX's C1; learned from augmentation in a network

Slide 24

AlexNet (2012): the first layer it learned

  • Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton (2012): eight layers, 60 million weights, backprop on 1.2 million labelled photographs, split over two GPUs
  • Its 96 first-layer filters, 11 × 11 × 3: one GPU's half learned mostly colour-free edges, the other's mostly colour blobs. No one designed them: training on labels alone produced Gabor-like filters, like V1 simple cells
Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton NeurIPS 2012

Slide 25

A mini-HMAX in PyTorch: the network you will train

class MiniHMAX(nn.Module): def __init__(self, num_classes): super().__init__() self.s1 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, 16, kernel_size=5, padding=2), nn.ReLU()) self.c1 = nn.MaxPool2d(4) # max over 4 × 4 blocks self.s2b = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(16, 64, kernel_size=7, padding=3), nn.ReLU()) self.c2b = nn.AdaptiveMaxPool2d(1) # max over all positions self.readout = nn.Linear(64, num_classes) def forward(self, images): # (batch, 3, 224, 224) hidden = self.s1(images) # (batch, 16, 224, 224) hidden = self.c1(hidden) # (batch, 16, 56, 56) hidden = self.s2b(hidden) # (batch, 64, 56, 56) pooled = self.c2b(hidden).flatten(1) # (batch, 64) return self.readout(pooled) # class scores (logits)
  • S: template matching (convolution, ReLU); C: a maximum over positions. HMAX's bypass route (the short path that skips the middle layers), S1 → C1 → S2b → C2b: the network you will train with Adam (the training lecture's optimizer), keeping the epoch with the best validation accuracy

Slide 26

ResNet-50: four block groups

  • ResNet-50 stacks 16 residual blocks (a few convolutions whose input is added back to their output) in four block groups, named layer1 to layer4 in PyTorch. Each group after the first halves the maps' height and width with a stride-2 convolution; the only max-pooling is right after conv1
  • layer4 is the last group: its 7 × 7 maps are averaged (global average pooling) into the image's feature vector, then a readout names the category
  • The ResNet-18 you recorded earlier uses the same names

Slide 27

Freeze the encoder, or fine-tune the last block group

  • The encoder is every layer before the readout. Frozen: its weights stay fixed. Fine-tuning: keep training some pretrained weights on the new task
  • Middle road: freeze conv1 to layer3, fine-tune layer4 and the readout
  • BatchNorm rescales each channel by the current minibatch's mean and variance in training mode, by stored averages in evaluation mode; freezing weights does not freeze those averages. model.eval() on new images; frozen layers back in eval mode while fine-tuning
Kumar, Raghunathan, Jones, Ma & Liang ICLR 2022

Explainability

Accuracy tells you that a network works, not how. Two networks with the same score can rely on completely different evidence.

The rest of today: which parts of the image did the answer depend on, and how would you test that claim? As in a psychophysics experiment: change the stimulus, measure the response, and run a control.

Slide 30

Occlusion: cover a region and watch the score

  • An accuracy score cannot say which part of an image drove a decision. To find out, change the image and watch the answer
  • Cover part of the image with a grey square, run the network again, and repeat at every position: an occlusion map of the correct class's probability
  • A drop can also come from the grey square itself. Compare with a control: a square of the same size placed off the bright region of the map; if the score drops there too, the square itself is doing the work
Zeiler & Fergus ECCV 2014

Slide 31

Ways to ask which pixels mattered

An attribution map (or heat map): each image region's contribution to one class's score: its logit (the class's score before the softmax) or its probability. The class can be the network's answer or any class you name.

  • All are local: they explain one decision on one image (what a unit does across all images is the next lecture). Today: occlusion, saliency, SmoothGrad; Grad-CAM briefly
  • Perturb and rerun: cover a region, measure the drop in the score: occlusion (previous slide)
  • Gradient at the pixels: how fast the score changes as each pixel changes: saliency; averaged over many gradients to steady it: integrated gradients, SmoothGrad
  • Gradient at the last feature maps: ask the last layer's coarse maps which of them push the score up, and where they fire: Grad-CAM

Occlusion asks what the decision loses without a region; saliency and Grad-CAM ask how the score would move if the pixels, or the top maps, changed a little.

Zeiler & Fergus ECCV 2014 · Simonyan, Vedaldi & Zisserman ICLR Workshop 2014 · Selvaraju et al. ICCV 2017

Slide 33

Saliency: the gradient at the pixels

𝐬  =  ∣ ∂zc∂𝐱 ∣\textcolor{#B23A2E}{\mathbf{s}} \;=\; \left|\,\frac{\partial \textcolor{#3F7A6B}{z_c}}{\partial \textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\mathbf{x}}}\,\right|

  • zc\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{z_c}: the logit for class cc, its score before the softmax; 𝐱\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\mathbf{x}}: the input image
  • 𝐬\textcolor{#B23A2E}{\mathbf{s}}: the saliency map, one value per pixel: how much a small change there moves the score
  • Computed by backpropagation, run to the input instead of to the weights: one forward pass, one backward pass, then the largest of the three colour channels per pixel. We draw the map from the logit and report the class probability
Simonyan, Vedaldi & Zisserman ICLR Workshop 2014

Slide 34

SmoothGrad, and integrated gradients

Computed: saliency, then SmoothGrad (50 noisy copies) · same network and photograph

  • SmoothGrad: add a little noise to the photograph, 50 times; take the saliency map each time; average. This is not a blur of one map. The gradient jumps wherever a ReLU switches on or off; averaging removes the jumps and keeps the regions that matter in every copy. A smoother map is not necessarily a more accurate one: that has to be tested
  • Integrated gradients: blend from a baseline (a blurred copy, or black) to the photograph and average the gradients along the way; not needed for your maps
Sundararajan, Taly & Yan ICML 2017 · Smilkov et al. ICML Workshop 2017

Grad-CAM: the gradient at the last feature maps

Slide 38

Recap

  • Simple and complex cells became convolution and max-pooling. The ventral stream: anatomy orders V1 → V2 → V4 → IT, cells prefer more complex things at each stage, responses start later at each stage
  • HMAX set the two operations from physiology and predicted data it was not fit to; networks then learned them from labels at scale
  • ResNet-50: residual blocks, BatchNorm (keep frozen layers in evaluation mode), fine-tune layer4; one architecture, three trainings (supervised, DINO, robust)
  • Attribution maps (occlusion, saliency, integrated gradients, SmoothGrad, Grad-CAM) say which pixels moved one class's score, for the class the network chose or any class you name
  • Edits with a control test a map: inpaint the region, compare with same-shape controls; deletion curves and sanity checks are the reading after the recap

Slide 42

Stride and padding set the size of a map

  • Stride ss: how far the filter moves between positions; stride 2 halves the map, so a unit of the next layer reads a wider patch of the image: the receptive field grows, as from V1 to IT
  • Padding pp: rows and columns of zeros around the input, so the filter can also sit on the border
  • Output size for a k×kk \times k filter: Hl=⌊(Hl−1+2p−k)/s⌋+1H_l = \lfloor (H_{l-1} + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1. ResNet-50's first layer: 224, k=7k = 7, s=2s = 2, p=3p = 3, gives 112

Slide 43

VGG (2014): small filters, stacked

  • Simonyan & Zisserman (2014) used only 3 × 3 filters, 16 to 19 layers deep. Three 3 × 3 filters in a row see the same 7 × 7 patch as one large filter, with 27 weights instead of 49 and two more nonlinearities
  • Deeper for less: 92.7% top-5 on ImageNet, and the first-layer filters still come out Gabor-like
Simonyan & Zisserman ICLR 2015

Slide 44

Deeper networks: add the input back

  • Stacking more plain layers made networks harder to train: a 56-layer network reached higher training error than a 20-layer one
  • A residual block computes a change F(A)\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{A}) and adds it to its input: output g(A+F(A))g(\boldsymbol{A} + \mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{A})). The added path is a skip connection
  • If a block is not needed, its change can stay near zero and the input passes through: networks of 50 to 152 layers trained well
  • Each convolution is followed by BatchNorm, explained on its own slide
He, Zhang, Ren & Sun CVPR 2016

Slide 45

Batch normalization

Computed: ResNet-50, 64 Imagenette photographs

  • As training changes the weights of earlier layers, the scale of each channel's values drifts. Batch normalization (BatchNorm) rescales every channel, layer after layer
  • For each channel: subtract the mean μB\mu_{\mathcal{B}} and divide by the standard deviation (s.d.) of the current minibatch (all its images and positions), then apply a learned scale γ\gamma and shift β\beta
  • z^=(z−μB) /σB2+ϵ,z~=γ z^+β\hat{z} = (z - \mu_{\mathcal{B}})\,/\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathcal{B}} + \epsilon}, \quad \tilde{z} = \gamma\,\hat{z} + \beta
Ioffe & Szegedy ICML 2015

Return to contents

Slide 46

Training mode and evaluation mode

  • Training mode normalizes with the minibatch's own statistics, and updates a running mean and variance: averages over the training batches
  • Evaluation mode (model.eval()) normalizes with those stored values instead
  • The stored values are not weights: no gradient changes them. model.eval() does not turn gradients off

Slide 47

In code: a saliency map

import torch from torchvision.models import resnet50, ResNet50_Weights model = resnet50(weights=ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1).eval() preprocess = ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1.transforms() x = preprocess(img).unsqueeze(0) # (1, 3, 224, 224) x.requires_grad_(True) # ask for the gradient at the pixels logits = model(x) # forward pass c = logits.argmax(dim=1) # predicted class (or any class you choose) logits[0, c].backward() # backprop the logit to the input saliency = x.grad.abs().amax(dim=1)[0] # max over colour channels -> (224, 224)

The same backward pass as training, run past the weights all the way back to the image 𝐱\mathbf{x}.

Slide 56

Catching the snow rule

  • The map: Ribeiro et al. (2016) trained a wolf-vs-husky classifier on wolves photographed in snow and huskies without; its explanation lit up the snow
  • The test: keep the animal, blur the snow. A segmentation mask marks the animal's pixels, so everything else can be edited. A rule based on the animal should keep its answer; a snow rule should flip
  • The control: blur a patch of the same size elsewhere, on the fur or on non-snow background. Only a flip under the first edit, and not the second, shows the snow did it
  • Same story in medicine, DeGrave et al. (2021): X-ray classifiers that read text markers and patient position instead of the lungs
Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin KDD 2016 · DeGrave, Janizek & Lee Nat Mach Intell 2021

Slide 57

What a map tells you, and what it does not

  • Tells you: a hypothesis about which region drove one decision, for any class; a fast way to spot a candidate shortcut (snow, a marker, a backdrop)
  • Does not tell you: proof. A map can survive random weights (guided methods) or miss what about a region mattered, as with texture
  • Faithfulness (does a map reflect what the model uses?) needs an intervention, as in the brain: a recording suggests what a neuron responds to; a lesion or a stimulation tests it
  • Usefulness to people is a separate question: maps helped people predict a biased husky-vs-wolf classifier, but no method helped people predict a network's errors on ImageNet photographs (Colin et al. 2022)

Make a map, turn it into an edit, compare with a control edit of the same size.

Slide 61

Sparse autoencoders find directions

Train a sparse autoencoder (SAE) on a network's internal activations 𝐚\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\mathbf{a}}:

𝐡=g(𝐖e 𝐚+𝐛e),𝐚^=𝐖d 𝐡+𝐛d,L=12∥𝐚^−𝐚∥2+λ ∥𝐡∥1\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\mathbf{h}} = g(\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{W}_e}\,\textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\mathbf{a}} + \mathbf{b}_e), \quad \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{W}_d}\,\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\mathbf{h}} + \mathbf{b}_d, \quad \mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\|\hat{\mathbf{a}} - \textcolor{#3E6D8E}{\mathbf{a}}\|^2 + \lambda\,\|\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\mathbf{h}}\|_1

  • An autoencoder rebuilds its input through a code 𝐡\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\mathbf{h}}; here the input is a network's activations, with many more code units than activations and few active at a time (the λ\lambda term: a penalty, λ\lambda its strength, on the sum of the absolute values of the code)
  • Each code unit is one direction; many are monosemantic (respond to one thing)
  • In a large language model, setting one feature to a high value by hand (a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature) made the model talk about the bridge
Bricken et al. Transformer Circuits Thread 2023 · Templeton et al. Transformer Circuits Thread 2024

Slide 62

Sparse autoencoders on vision models

  • CLIP (a network trained to match images with their captions): SAE features on its vision network can be turned up or down by hand (steered), and there are many more of them than units (Joseph et al. 2025a)
  • People judge SAE features of CLIP more single-meaning than its units, and switching on one SAE feature changes what a chatbot built on CLIP says about an image (Pach et al. 2025)
  • Open toolkits such as Prisma (Joseph et al. 2025b) make vision SAEs routine: a route for a final project
