Regularization 1: a penalty on the weights

L(θ)  =  Ltrain(θ)⏟fit  +    λ∥θ∥2⏟strength    ∥θ∥2⏟size of the parameters\mathcal{L}(\textcolor{#B5396B}{\boldsymbol\theta}) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(\textcolor{#B5396B}{\boldsymbol\theta})}_{\text{fit}} \;+\;\; \underbrace{\lambda\vphantom{\lVert\textcolor{#B5396B}{\boldsymbol\theta}\rVert^2}}_{\text{strength}}\;\;\underbrace{\lVert\textcolor{#B5396B}{\boldsymbol\theta}\rVert^2}_{\text{size of the parameters}}

Slide 7

Regularization 2: dropout

  • Dropout: at each training step, switch off a random half of the hidden units; all are on at test time
  • Often with the weight penalty, sometimes L1 (absolute values): each cuts the freedom to fit noise
Srivastava et al. JMLR 2014

Regularization 3: more examples

Computed: the same curve and noise, after Bishop (2006), Fig. 1.6

  • Last lecture: the same degree-9 polynomial fitted to 10, 15, 30 and 100 points; test RMSE 0.86 → 0.31
  • With 10 points it passes through every one; with 100 it cannot, and follows the curve instead

Slide 12

Core object recognition: the behavior to explain

  • A photograph flashed for 20 ms: is there an animal in it? People are 94% correct, and their frontal EEG separates animal from non-animal images by 150 ms
  • Too fast for eye movements or many back-and-forth loops: recognition rides mostly on one feedforward sweep through the visual system
  • Monkeys do the same task, and activity in their ventral stream carries the category early enough to drive the fast responses
Thorpe, Fize & Marlot Nature 1996 · Cauchoix, Crouzet, Fize & Serre NeuroImage 2016

Slide 13

The ventral stream: a hierarchy of areas

  • Retina → LGN → V1 → V2 → V4 → IT: each area responds later, and its receptive fields (the part of the visual field a neuron responds to) are larger
  • Tuning gets more complex (edges, parts, objects) and more tolerant of position and size
  • In IT, a linear readout of ~100 neurons reports the category: untangling, measured
DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust Neuron 2012 · Hung, Kreiman, Poggio & DiCarlo Science 2005

Slide 14

From the retina to V1, in one slide

  • About 100 million photoreceptors feed about 1 million ganglion cells: the eye compresses first
  • Ganglion and LGN cells: a center–surround template (left) that reports local contrast
  • V1 simple cells: a Gabor template (right), light and dark stripes inside a smooth window, that reports an edge at one orientation
Kuffler J Neurophysiol 1953 · Hubel & Wiesel J Physiol 1962

Slide 15

Simple and complex cells

Computed on model cells

  • Simple cell: an oriented template at one position. It needs the right pattern in the right place, an "AND" of its inputs: selectivity
  • Complex cell: the same orientation, anywhere in a larger field. It behaves like the maximum over simple cells at nearby positions, an "OR": invariance to position
  • Max-like pooling has been measured: in cat V1 complex cells and in macaque V4
Hubel & Wiesel J Physiol 1962 · Riesenhuber & Poggio Nat Neurosci 1999 · Lampl et al. J Neurophysiol 2004 · Gawne & Martin J Neurophysiol 2002

Slide 19

Selectivity, then invariance, repeated

  • Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980) and HMAX (Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999) alternate template matching (S, simple) and pooling (C, complex; HMAX takes the maximum), up to IT
  • At each step, receptive fields grow; units get more selective and more tolerant
  • HMAX's filters are set by hand or copied from natural images; only its top stage learns from labels. On rapid animal categorization it did about as well as people
Fukushima Biol Cybern 1980 · Riesenhuber & Poggio Nat Neurosci 1999 · Serre, Oliva & Poggio PNAS 2007

Slide 21

A neuron is a feature detector: template matching

  • Among inputs of the same length ∥𝐱∥\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert, 𝐰⊤𝐱\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} is largest when 𝐱\mathbf{x} looks like the weights 𝐰\mathbf{w}, so 𝐰\mathbf{w} acts as a stored template
  • All inputs have length 1, so 𝐰⊤𝐱\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} is the cosine of the angle to 𝐰\mathbf{w}; ReLU clips negatives to 0
  • Set the weights by hand to a pattern → the neuron becomes a detector for that pattern

Slide 22

Why not a dense layer on the pixels? Count the weights

  • Flatten a 224 × 224 colour image into a vector: one dense unit (one weight per input value) needs 224 · 224 · 3 = 150,528 weights, and it ignores which pixels are neighbours
  • AlexNet's first layer instead learns 96 small filters of 11 × 11 × 3 and reuses each one at every position: 34,848 weights, about 400 times fewer than 96 dense units
  • Local filters, the same everywhere: the simple cell's template, copied across the visual field
Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton NeurIPS 2012

Slide 23

Convolution: one filter, slid over the image

Computed on the tiger photograph of the first lecture

  • A filter (or kernel: a template, as on the template-matching slide) is a small grid of weights. At each position, multiply by the pixels under it and sum; a ReLU sets negatives to 0
  • The result is a feature map: how well each part of the image matches the filter
  • Same weights everywhere: shift the image, and the map shifts with it (equivariance)

Slide 25

Stride and padding set the size of a map

  • Stride ss: how far the filter moves between positions. Stride 2 skips every other position, so the map is about half as tall and half as wide
  • Padding pp: rows and columns of zeros around the input, so the filter can also sit on the border
  • Output size for a k×kk \times k filter: Hl=⌊(Hl−1+2p−k)/s⌋+1H_l = \lfloor (H_{l-1} + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1. ResNet-50's first layer: 224, k=7k = 7, s=2s = 2, p=3p = 3, gives 112

Slide 26

Pooling: from a map to fewer numbers

  • Max-pooling keeps the largest value in each small window: the complex cell's "OR", which tolerates small shifts
  • Global average pooling averages each feature map over all its positions: one number per channel. These numbers, one per channel, form the image's feature vector, the readout's input
  • Two ways to shrink a map: pooling, or a stride-2 convolution. Many modern networks use mostly the second, then one global average
Lin, Chen & Yan ICLR 2014

Slide 27

A mini-HMAX in PyTorch

class MiniHMAX(nn.Module): def __init__(self, num_classes): super().__init__() self.s1 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, 16, kernel_size=5, padding=2), nn.ReLU()) self.c1 = nn.MaxPool2d(4) # max over 4 × 4 blocks self.s2b = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(16, 64, kernel_size=7, padding=3), nn.ReLU()) self.c2b = nn.AdaptiveMaxPool2d(1) # max over all positions self.readout = nn.Linear(64, num_classes) def forward(self, images): # (batch, 3, 224, 224) hidden = self.s1(images) # (batch, 16, 224, 224) hidden = self.c1(hidden) # (batch, 16, 56, 56) hidden = self.s2b(hidden) # (batch, 64, 56, 56) pooled = self.c2b(hidden).flatten(1) # (batch, 64) return self.readout(pooled) # class logits
  • S: template matching (convolution, ReLU); C: a maximum over positions. HMAX's bypass route, S1 → C1 → S2b → C2b: the network you will train, with Adam, then a cosine schedule (CosineAnnealingLR), keeping the best validation epoch

Slide 30

Deeper networks: add the input back

  • Stacking more plain layers made networks harder to train: a 56-layer network reached higher training error than a 20-layer one
  • A residual block computes a change F(A)\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{A}) and adds it to its input: output g(A+F(A))g(\boldsymbol{A} + \mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{A})). The added path is a skip connection
  • If a block is not needed, its change can stay near zero and the input passes through: networks of 50 to 152 layers trained well
  • Each convolution is followed by BatchNorm, explained two slides on
He, Zhang, Ren & Sun CVPR 2016

Slide 31

ResNet-50: four block groups

  • ResNet-50 stacks 16 residual blocks in four block groups, named layer1 to layer4 in PyTorch. Each group after the first starts by halving the maps' height and width
  • layer4 is the last group: its 7 × 7 maps are averaged (global average pooling) into the image's feature vector, then a readout names the category
  • The ResNet-18 you recorded earlier in the course uses the same names; its layer1, layer3 and layer4 were the early, middle and late stages

Slide 32

Batch normalization

Computed: ResNet-50, 64 Imagenette photographs

  • As training changes the weights of earlier layers, the scale of each channel's values drifts. Batch normalization (BatchNorm) rescales every channel, layer after layer
  • For each channel: subtract the mean μB\mu_{\mathcal{B}} and divide by the standard deviation (s.d.) of the current minibatch (all its images and positions), then apply a learned scale γ\gamma and shift β\beta
  • z^=(z−μB) /σB2+ϵ,z~=γ z^+β\hat{z} = (z - \mu_{\mathcal{B}})\,/\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathcal{B}} + \epsilon}, \quad \tilde{z} = \gamma\,\hat{z} + \beta
Ioffe & Szegedy ICML 2015

Return to contents

Slide 33

Training mode and evaluation mode

  • Training mode normalizes with the minibatch's own statistics, and updates a running mean and variance: averages over the training batches
  • Evaluation mode (model.eval()) normalizes with those stored values instead
  • The stored values are not weights: no gradient changes them. model.eval() does not turn gradients off

Slide 35

Freeze the encoder, or fine-tune the last block group

  • The encoder is every layer before the readout. Frozen: its weights stay fixed. Fine-tuning: keep training some pretrained weights on the new task
  • A middle road between a new readout on frozen features and backprop into every layer: freeze conv1 to layer3; fine-tune layer4 and the readout
  • Freezing the weights does not freeze BatchNorm: in model.train() the frozen layers' running statistics still change. Keep their BatchNorm in evaluation mode
Kumar, Raghunathan, Jones, Ma & Liang ICLR 2022

Slide 36

Which layers transfer to a new task?

  • Yosinski et al. (2014): train on 500 ImageNet categories, copy the first nn layers into a network for the other 500, train the rest
  • Copied and frozen (red): two layers did as well as training from scratch; more hurt, most at the top. Early features are general, late ones specific
  • Copied and fine-tuned (pink): better than from scratch at every depth
Yosinski, Clune, Bengio & Lipson NeurIPS 2014 · Razavian, Azizpour, Sullivan & Carlsson CVPRW 2014

Slide 37

A vision transformer trained without labels

  • A vision transformer (ViT) cuts the image into 16 × 16-pixel patches, one token each; every token exchanges information with every other
  • One extra token, the class token (CLS), gathers from all patches; its final value is the feature vector
  • DINO trains it without labels (self-supervised): two crops of one photograph must get matching features. It trains ResNets the same way: the DINO ResNet-50 you read out in the second assignment
Dosovitskiy et al. ICLR 2021 · Caron et al. ICCV 2021 · Siméoni et al. arXiv 2025

What does a network rely on? Texture or shape

  • Recall the generalization lecture: an ImageNet-trained ResNet-50 calls a cat with elephant skin "elephant"; people go by the shape (Geirhos et al. 2019). Training on images repainted in random textures made it rely more on shape: what a network relies on depends on its training images
  • An accuracy score cannot say which part of an image drove a decision. To find out, change the image and watch the answer: the next slide
Geirhos et al. ICLR 2019 · Baker, Lu, Erlikhman & Kellman PLOS Comput Biol 2018

Slide 40

Recap

  • Generalize better: a penalty on the weights (weight decay, ridge), more or altered data, dropout; choose the penalty on validation data
  • Brain: center–surround, then oriented simple cells, then complex cells that pool them; repeated up to IT, where a linear readout reports the object
  • V1 without labels: a sparse code of natural images learns oriented filters like simple cells, but longer
  • Network: filters shared across positions (convolution), stride and pooling to shrink the maps, global average pooling to a feature vector, a readout. Its first layer learns V1-like filters
  • Deep and trainable: residual blocks and BatchNorm (training mode uses batch statistics, evaluation mode the stored ones)
  • Reuse: freeze the encoder, or fine-tune its last block group (layer4); self-supervised training (DINO) gives features without labels. Check what it relies on: texture bias, occlusion
