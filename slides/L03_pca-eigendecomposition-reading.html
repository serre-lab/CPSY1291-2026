Slide 6

Why look for a direction at all?

  • Along the long arrow, both pixels brighten or darken together. Its weights are close to (1,1)/2(1,1)/\sqrt{2}, so it behaves like their average and accounts for 90% of the variation.
  • Along the short arrow, one pixel brightens as the other darkens. Its weights are close to (1,−1)/2(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}, so it compares the two pixels and accounts for the remaining 10%.
  • For this pixel pair, PCA learned directions close to average and difference.

Slide 8

From observed values to a Gaussian model

  • The previous histogram showed observed pixel values. This is an idealized reference model, not an assumption PCA requires.
  • A Gaussian is smooth and bell-shaped. Its mean μ\mu gives the center and its standard deviation σ\sigma gives the spread; about 68% lies between μ−σ\mu-\sigma and μ+σ\mu+\sigma.

Slide 9

The Gaussian model in two dimensions

  • Each simulated point contains two measurements, (x1,x2)(x_1,x_2). These are draws from the model, not the 120 photographs.
  • The mean becomes a mean vector μ\boldsymbol{\mu}, the center of the cloud; the covariance matrix describes its width, elongation and tilt.
  • PCA will find the ellipse's principal axes.

Slide 10

Where is the cloud, and how does it spread?

For each pixel jj, average over the n=120n=120 images:

  • Mean: μj=1n∑ixj(i)\displaystyle \mu_j=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_j^{(i)}
    The cloud’s center is μ=(μ1,μ2)\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_1,\mu_2).
  • Variance: σj2=1n∑i(xj(i)−μj)2\displaystyle \sigma_j^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\bigl(x_j^{(i)}-\mu_j\bigr)^2
    Spread around the mean along that coordinate.
  • Standard deviation: σj=σj2\sigma_j=\sqrt{\sigma_j^2}
    Spread in pixel-intensity units.

Slide 11

Where is the cloud, and how does it spread?

  • So subtract the mean from every point: 𝐱~(i)=𝐱(i)−μ\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)} = \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}. This is centering, and it slides the cloud until its centre sits at the origin.
  • Everything from here on describes variation around the mean.
  • A negative centered value means “darker than this pixel’s average,” not a negative raw intensity.

Slide 12

Centering, in code

mean = features.mean(axis=0) # (D,) mean response across stimuli, for each coordinate centred = features - mean # (N, D) each stimulus response minus the mean response
  • features is the response matrix from the first lectures: one row per stimulus (images here), one column per coordinate.
  • The mean is a vector with one entry per coordinate. Subtracting it from every row leaves each column summing to zero.
  • Keep the mean. Everything computed from here on is a difference from it, so we will want it back at the end.

Slide 13

Covariance: do the deviations go together?

  • These are the same centered responses: x~1=x1−μ1\tilde{x}_1=x_1-\mu_1 and x~2=x2−μ2\tilde{x}_2=x_2-\mu_2. Same signs give a positive product; opposite signs give a negative product.
  • Covariance is their average product:
    cov⁡(x~1,x~2)=1n∑ix~1(i)x~2(i)\displaystyle \operatorname{cov}(\tilde{x}_1,\tilde{x}_2)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\tilde{x}_1^{(i)}\tilde{x}_2^{(i)}.
  • Pearson correlation is covariance measured in standard-deviation units:
    r=cov⁡(x~1,x~2)σ1σ2\displaystyle r=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\tilde{x}_1,\tilde{x}_2)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}.
  • Two consequences lead directly to the covariance matrix:
    cov⁡(x~1,x~1)=var⁡(x1)=σ12\displaystyle \operatorname{cov}(\tilde{x}_1,\tilde{x}_1)=\operatorname{var}(x_1)=\sigma_1^2,
    cov⁡(x~2,x~1)=cov⁡(x~1,x~2)\displaystyle \operatorname{cov}(\tilde{x}_2,\tilde{x}_1)=\operatorname{cov}(\tilde{x}_1,\tilde{x}_2).

Slide 14

The covariance matrix

  • In 𝐂\mathbf{C}, the diagonal contains the two variances; the off-diagonal contains their covariance.
  • Geometrically 𝐂\mathbf{C} summarizes the cloud’s spread and orientation: round, an ellipse, or an ellipse tilted because the two vary together.
  • The ellipse's long axis — its direction of greatest variance — is the direction we have been looking for.

Slide 21

Finding the best direction from covariance

  • We found that direction by turning a line and watching the leftovers. The covariance matrix gives it to us directly.
  • The variance of the projections along a direction 𝐰\mathbf{w} is 𝐰⊤𝐂𝐰\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}. Finding the best direction means making that quantity as large as possible.
  • Multiply any unit vector 𝐮\mathbf{u} by 𝐂\mathbf{C}. The result 𝐂𝐮\mathbf{Cu} points somewhere else: the matrix has turned the direction.

Slide 23

What is an eigenvector?

  • Two directions are the exception: 𝐂\mathbf{C} sends them straight back along themselves, only longer or shorter. They are the eigenvectors 𝐯k\mathbf{v}_k, and the factor is the eigenvalue λk\lambda_k: 𝐂𝐯k=λk𝐯k\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k\mathbf{v}_k.
  • Here the long one sits at 44° — the angle we turned the line to by hand.
  • The direction that makes 𝐰⊤𝐂𝐰\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w} largest is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. That is why they turned up: they answer the question we were turning the line to solve.

Return to contents

Slide 24

The eigenvalue is variance along that direction

  • An eigenvector 𝐯k\mathbf{v}_k gives a direction; its eigenvalue λk\lambda_k gives the variance along it.
  • Rank them by eigenvalue and take them in order: largest first, then the perpendicular direction with the next largest, and so on.
  • Ranked this way the eigenvectors of 𝐂\mathbf{C} are the principal components, the first is PC1, and computing them is principal component analysis (PCA).

Slide 25

From a line to a plane

  • Keep two directions and describe each point by two coordinates.
  • Keeping all directions changes the coordinate system without losing information.
  • Keeping fewer directions replaces each point by its projection onto a line, a plane, or a higher-dimensional subspace.

Slide 26

The new coordinates are the PC scores

For one image, take its dot product with each retained direction:

zk=𝐯k⊤(𝐱−μ).z_k=\mathbf{v}_k^\top(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}).

  • A direction 𝐯k\mathbf{v}_k gives weights on the original measurements.
  • A score zkz_k is this image's coordinate along that direction.
  • Keeping KK directions gives KK numbers per image. These are the coordinates we plot or give to a readout.

Slide 27

Reconstruction: the way back

  • Start from the mean.
  • Add each retained direction, weighted by the image's score:

𝐱^=μ+∑k=1Kzk𝐯k.\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_k\mathbf{v}_k.

  • The residual is the difference between the original and reconstructed measurements.

Slide 36

The measurement scale changes PCA

  • PCA maximizes variance, so a feature measured in millimetres dominates one measured in metres: in raw units PC1 is 1.00 x1+0.00 x21.00\,x_1 + 0.00\,x_2 and the second feature is invisible.
  • After z-scoring each feature, PC1 is 0.71 x1+0.71 x20.71\,x_1 + 0.71\,x_2 and recovers the diagonal structure the two features share.
  • Standardize when the units are not comparable. When they are comparable and the variances differ for real reasons, standardizing changes the relative weighting of features — it is a modelling decision, not a default.
The same 220 points, raw and z-scored

Slide 52

PCA = eigendecomposition of 𝐂\mathbf{C}

Covariance (centered, nn samples):   𝐂=1n∑i𝐱~(i)𝐱~(i)⊤\;\mathbf{C} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)\top}   (code divides by n−1n-1; same eigenvectors)

Eigendecomposition (orthonormal 𝐕\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{V}}):

𝐂=𝐕 Λ 𝐕⊤,𝐂 𝐯k=λk 𝐯k,λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λd\mathbf{C} = \textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{V}}\,\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\,\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{V}}^\top,\qquad \mathbf{C}\,\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{v}_k} = \textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\lambda_k}\,\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{v}_k},\qquad \textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\lambda_1}\ge\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\lambda_2}\ge\cdots\ge\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\lambda_d}

  • Principal axes = eigenvectors 𝐯k\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{v}_k} of 𝐂\mathbf{C}.
  • Variance along 𝐯k\textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{v}_k} = λk\textcolor{#3F7A6B}{\lambda_k}.
  • PC1=𝐯1\text{PC1} = \textcolor{#B5396B}{\mathbf{v}_1}, the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.

Slide 53

PCA in code: two routes to the same answer

centred = features - features.mean(axis=0) # Route 1: eigendecomposition of sample covariance C = np.cov(centred.T) lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(C) # ascending order lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1] # descending order # Route 2: SVD, without forming C _, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(centred, full_matrices=False) lam2 = s**2 / (len(centred) - 1) V2 = Vt.T # directions as columns Z = centred @ V2[:, :K] # (n, K) scores
  • Use eigh, not eig: 𝐂\mathbf{C} is symmetric, so the eigenvalues are real and eigh is faster and stabler. It returns them ascending, so the reversal is not optional.
  • Prefer route 2. Forming 𝐂\mathbf{C} costs D×DD\times D memory; for a 256×256256\times256 color image D≈196,608D\approx196{,}608 and 𝐂\mathbf{C} would be ~309 GB in float64. SVD never builds it. Sign is arbitrary: 𝐯k\mathbf{v}_k and −𝐯k-\mathbf{v}_k are both valid; Assignment 2's helper fixes it so the largest entry is positive.

Two views, one answer

  • Maximize the variance of the projections: max⁡∥𝐯∥=1𝐯⊤𝐂 𝐯\displaystyle\max_{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=1}\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{v}
  • Minimize the squared reconstruction error: min⁡∥𝐯∥=11n∑i∥𝐱~(i)−(𝐯⊤𝐱~(i))𝐯∥2\displaystyle\min_{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=1}\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)} - (\mathbf{v}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)})\mathbf{v}\rVert^2
  • These are the same problem: total variance is fixed, so whatever the projection captures, the residual loses. Both are solved by the top eigenvector of 𝐂\mathbf{C}.
  • For KK retained PCs, the average squared fitting-data reconstruction error is ∑k>Kλk\sum_{k>K}\lambda_k (using covariance divided by nn).
  • Connection to MDS: classical MDS on a Euclidean dissimilarity matrix returns the PCA coordinates up to an orthogonal transformation of the centered data. MDS was doing an eigendecomposition all along — it just never told you which axis was which.

Slide 60

PCA as decorrelation & whitening

  • Project onto the eigenbasis and the new coordinates are uncorrelated — the covariance becomes diagonal. For positive eigenvalues, rescale each retained axis by 1/λi1/\sqrt{\lambda_i} and you get whitening: zero correlation, unit variance.

𝐂=𝐕Λ𝐕⊤    ⇒    𝐳=Λ−1/2𝐕⊤(𝐱−μ),Cov(𝐳)=𝐈\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^\top \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\mathbf{V}^\top(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}), \quad \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{I}

  • Why analysts reach for it:
    • remove redundancy between measured dimensions before modeling
    • decorrelate inputs, without assuming the resulting features are independent
    • compress by dropping directions; whether this denoises depends on what they contain

Slide 63

Identity and viewpoint contribute variance

For equal numbers of views per object:

Vtotal=Vwithin+Vbetween.V_{\mathrm{total}}=V_{\mathrm{within}}+V_{\mathrm{between}}.

  • Within: average variation around each object's mean.
  • Between: variation of object means around the overall mean.
  • Example: total 5=1+45=1+4. Within 20%20\%; between 80%80\%.

Slide 65

Preprocessing neural responses

  • Missing responses: drop units with too many missing values, then replace remaining missing values by that unit's observed mean; or drop every incomplete unit.
  • Centering: subtract each unit's mean across images.
  • Standardizing: also divide each unit by its standard deviation. A unit with standard deviation zero needs separate handling.
  • Raw-response PCA gives high-variance units more influence. Z-scored PCA puts units on comparable scales.

Example: standard deviations 22 and 2020 imply variances 44 and 400400. After z-scoring, both variances are 11.

Slide 67

PCA only ever finds a flat subspace

  • PCA can only find a flat subspace. If the data lie on a curved manifold — a spiral, a Swiss roll, a torus — a low-dimensional linear projection can fold distinct regions onto each other.
  • Two points far apart along the manifold can land on top of each other after projection: the two arms are perfectly distinct in the plane and inseparable along PC1.
  • t-SNE and UMAP construct nonlinear maps emphasizing local relationships. Their displays can distort global structure and need their own checks.
